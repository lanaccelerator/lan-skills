## Chesbrough Open Innovation Framework

**Source:** Chesbrough, H. (2003). "A Better Way to Innovate." Harvard Business Review.
https://hbr.org/2003/07/a-better-way-to-innovate

The canonical framework for understanding how corporations access and deploy innovation
from external sources. Three models:

| Model | Description | Best For |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| **Outside-In** | Absorbing external knowledge, technology, and IP into the corporation | Speed-to-market; accessing capabilities the corporation cannot build internally in the required timeline |
| **Inside-Out** | Licensing internal IP, spinning out technology, or selling unused knowledge assets | Monetizing dormant R&D; reducing innovation lab costs; creating startup ecosystem alignment |
| **Coupled** | Alliance-based co-development with external partners (startups, universities, other corporations) | Shared-risk innovation; co-development where both parties contribute differentially |

**Key Chesbrough insight (2003, updated 2020):** "The boundary of the firm is becoming
more permeable. Valuable ideas can come from inside or outside the company, and can go
to market from inside or outside the company as well." The four-mode framework
(build/buy/partner/license) operationalizes this permeability.

## Corporate Innovation Program Benchmarks (BCG 2024)

**Source:** BCG 2024 Corporate Innovation Survey — 1,200 global innovation executives.
https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/corporate-innovation-benchmarking

| Metric | Value | Implication |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| Fortune 500 with formal innovation program | 84% | Almost all large corporations have programs; differentiation is in execution, not existence |
| Of those, reporting measurable strategic impact | 23% | 77% have innovation theater, not innovation outcomes |
| Global pilot-to-scale conversion (average) | 12% | Baseline expectation: 88% of pilots fail to scale |
| Top-quartile corporate innovators: pilot-to-scale | 35% | 3× the average — achievable with named champions and kill criteria |
| "Not invented here" syndrome: % of pilot failures in manufacturing | 40% | Cultural resistance is the leading cause of failure in manufacturing sector |
| Time from pilot approval to first payment to startup (LATAM median) | 4–6 months | Procurement is the primary startup partnership killer; US equivalent: 6–8 weeks |
| Time from pilot approval to first payment to startup (US median) | 6–8 weeks | LATAM is 8–16× slower — budget accordingly |

**Key implication for LATAM corporate teams:** The 23% strategic impact rate is a
*global* average. LATAM pilot success rate is ~18% *(BCG 2024, LATAM subset)* —
lower due to procurement friction, internal champion deficit, and FX risk affecting
pilot ROI. The gap between 18% and top-quartile 35% is not talent — it is process:
named champions, defined kill criteria, and MSA templates prepared before startup engagement.

## Innovation Theater Benchmarks (NESTA 2023)

**Source:** NESTA Open Innovation Toolkit + NESTA LATAM Corporate Innovation Study 2023.
https://www.nesta.org.uk/toolkit/open-innovation-toolkit/

| Pattern | Failure Rate | Notes |
|---------|-------------|-------|
| Pilots without named C-level champion | 3× higher failure rate | A VP of Innovation reporting to a committee is not a champion |
| Pilots without defined success criteria | 2.4× higher failure rate | "We'll know it when we see it" produces theater, not data |
| Pilots without defined kill criteria | Rarely killed — budget consumed | Open-ended pilots consume budget without producing decisions |
| First-time corporate-startup partnerships | 2× higher friction/failure vs. experienced corporates | No prior MSA template, no startup relationship manager, procurement mismatch |
| Pilots with <$100k budget in LATAM enterprise | Cannot cover procurement + legal + onboarding | $50k is consumed before pilot starts; meaningful LATAM enterprise pilots require $150k–$500k |

**NESTA definition of innovation theater:** "Innovation activities that signal commitment
to innovation without producing any measurable change in capability, revenue, or operations.
Characterized by hackathons, demo days, and accelerator partnerships that produce press
releases and no commercial agreements."

## McKinsey "Not Invented Here" Syndrome (2024)

**Source:** McKinsey & Company (2024). "Innovation Culture: Why Good Ideas Die Inside
Large Companies." McKinsey Quarterly.
https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/innovation-culture

40% of pilot failures in manufacturing sector attributed to internal resistance after
proof-of-concept — i.e., the pilot works technically but internal adoption fails due to
organizational resistance. This is the "Not Invented Here" (NIH) syndrome — employees
resist external solutions that threaten internal teams' relevance.

**NIH mitigation pattern (McKinsey 2024):**
1. Co-develop the pilot specification with the internal team that will eventually adopt it
2. Frame the startup as a vendor/tool, not a replacement for the internal team
3. Name an internal adoption champion (separate from the innovation champion) whose
   performance review includes successful startup adoption metrics

## Gartner Digital Transformation 2025 — Innovation Quadrant

**Source:** Gartner 2025 Digital Transformation Framework.
https://www.gartner.com/en/information-technology/topics/digital-transformation

Key data for open innovation evaluation:

- **Digital adoption by sector (LATAM):**
  - Fintech / Financial Services: 85% digitized
  - Telecommunications: 72% digitized
  - Retail / CPG: 45% digitized
  - Manufacturing: 28% digitized
  - Agribusiness: 22% digitized
  *(Source: IDB Digital Transformation Report 2024)*

- **Implication:** Lower digitization = larger open innovation opportunity AND higher
  implementation complexity. A manufacturing corporate pursuing AI-driven quality control
  (28% digitized sector) faces fundamentally different complexity than a fintech pursuing
  AI decisioning (85% digitized).

- **Gartner "Pace Layers" for technology adoption:** Core systems (ERP, core banking)
  change every 20 years. Differentiation systems (data analytics, customer platforms)
  change every 5 years. Innovation systems (AI, new product pilots) change every 1–3 years.
  Open innovation operates in the "Innovation" layer — startups should never touch core
  systems in a pilot.

## LATAM Corporate Venturing Data (LAVCA 2025)

**Source:** LAVCA 2025 Startup Ecosystem Insights.
https://www.lavca.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/LAVCA_Startup-Ecosystem-Insights_2025.pdf

- Corporates/CVCs represent ~25% of LATAM LP base *(LAVCA 2025)* — one of the highest
  corporate LP concentrations globally (US: ~10%)
- Brazilian corporates lead LATAM CVC activity: Itaú (IVC), TOTVS Ventures, Embraer-X,
  and Ambev (Backer) each with active arms; combined portfolio >80 startups
- Colombian CVC: Bancolombia DF, Grupo Bolívar, EPM
- Mexican CVC: FEMSA Ventures, BBVA Spark, Telcel/AMX Ventures
- LATAM pilot success rate: ~18% *(BCG 2024, LATAM subset)* vs. 25% globally
- Hub model (Cubo Itaú, Wayra Brazil, Ruta N Medellín) outperforms internal labs in
  startup sourcing: 2.5× more qualified startups *(NESTA LATAM 2023)*
