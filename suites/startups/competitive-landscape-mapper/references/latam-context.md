## 1. US Incumbent LATAM Gap

**The structural window:** Enterprise US software companies (Workday, Salesforce,
SAP, ServiceNow, etc.) have almost no LATAM-specific sales motion for SMEs.
Their LATAM revenue comes from large enterprises and government. SME segments
in LATAM are systematically underserved.

**Why this exists:** US software was built for US enterprise buyers — English UX,
USD pricing, US compliance requirements, US payment methods. Localizing for
LATAM SMEs requires Portuguese/Spanish native UX, local payment methods (PIX/OXXO/SPEI),
local regulatory compliance (CFDI/NF-e/e-Social), and a local sales force with SME
relationship-building norms. US incumbents rarely make this investment for SME segments.

**The window duration:** 3–5 years for established US incumbents to decide to localize
and execute on it. LATAM-native startups that achieve distribution lock-in (switching costs,
data moats) before this window closes build a defensible position. After the window closes,
the risk is capital competition: US incumbents can outspend on customer acquisition.

**How to document this in competitive analysis:** Name the specific US incumbent, document
exactly where they fall short (language, pricing, compliance, support), and quantify the
distribution advantage the local startup has. "We know the market better" is not sufficient —
name the specific capability gap.

## 2. LATAM-First Advantage Dimensions

These are structural advantages that a LATAM-native startup has over any foreign competitor
entering the same market:

**Spanish/Portuguese UX:** Not just language translation — the entire product interaction
model. Error messages in Spanish that make sense to a Mexican SME owner. Onboarding flows
that match how LATAM business owners learn software (mobile-first, WhatsApp support).

**Local payment methods:** PIX (Brazil), OXXO (Mexico), SPEI (Mexico), PSE (Colombia),
Khipu (Chile). B2B SaaS that bills only in credit card or wire transfer loses SME customers
who rely on local payment methods. Integration is a technical moat that takes 3–6 months
to build properly.

**Local invoicing compliance:**
- **Mexico:** CFDI 4.0 (SAT digital invoicing mandate). Non-compliant software cannot
  be used for any transaction in Mexico. Compliance built-in = switching cost.
- **Brazil:** NF-e (Nota Fiscal Eletrônica) for goods, NFS-e for services. SPED compliance
  for accounting. e-Social for labor. Each has its own API, certificate requirements, and audit trail.
- **Colombia:** Factura electrónica (DIAN mandate since 2019). Active habilitation required.
- **Argentina:** AFIP digital invoicing. FE (Factura Electrónica) for all taxpayer categories.

**Local sales network:** LATAM B2B sales is relationship-driven. Cold outbound (email sequences,
LinkedIn) has lower conversion than warm introductions through industry associations, chambers
of commerce, and peer networks. A local founder with existing relationships in the target
industry has a first-year distribution advantage that capital cannot buy.

## 3. Regulatory Moat Examples by Sector and Country

Regulatory moats are more achievable in LATAM than in the US — market fragmentation and
regulatory complexity create structural barriers that US incumbents rarely invest in crossing.

**Fintech — Mexico:**
- SOFOM (Sociedad Financiera de Objeto Múltiple): Required for any credit product.
  Approval timeline: 12–24 months through CNBV. This is a structural entry barrier.
- SPEI connectivity: Real-time payment network access requires CNBV authorization.
  A fintech with SPEI integration has a capability competitors must wait 12+ months to replicate.

**Fintech — Colombia:**
- SFC (Superintendencia Financiera): Supervision required for credit and deposit products.
  Sandbox available but limited spots — first movers in the sandbox gain regulatory familiarity.

**Fintech — Brazil:**
- BCB (Banco Central do Brasil): Payment institution license required for any payment product.
  PIX integration requires BCB certification. Pix Garantido (Pix credit) requires additional approval.

**Health Data — Brazil:**
- LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados): Brazil's GDPR. For health data, additional
  anonymization requirements. Companies that process patient data must have a DPO (Data
  Protection Officer) and documented data processing procedures. This creates compliance
  switching costs — migrating to a competitor requires re-establishing data processing agreements.

**Labor Compliance — Brazil:**
- e-Social: Mandatory labor compliance reporting for employers with >10 employees.
  Companies using software for e-Social reporting face high switching costs —
  historical filings are stored in the system and auditors may need to access prior reports.

**E-invoicing (LATAM-wide):**
- Any company that processes electronic invoices on behalf of customers becomes a
  compliance dependency. Switching to a competitor means migrating invoice history
  and re-certifying with the tax authority. This is a structural switching cost.

## 4. Competitive Intelligence Sources for LATAM

When building competitor profiles, use these LATAM-specific sources. Do not rely solely
on Crunchbase or TechCrunch — most LATAM funding rounds are not covered in US media.

**Funding and portfolio data:**
- LAVCA (lavca.org): Annual VC report with portfolio maps, top investors, and deal data
- Latitud Scout (latitud.com/scout): LATAM startup database with funding rounds, investors
- Crunchbase LATAM filter: Search by country, sector, funding stage. Filter to BR, MX, CO, AR, CL, PE
- LinkedIn: Funding announcements, employee growth (hiring = growth signal), leadership changes

**Press and deal announcements:**
- Contxto (contxto.com): LATAM tech news in English and Spanish
- Forbes Brazil (forbesbrasil.com.br): Brazilian startup coverage
- El Economista / Expansión (Mexico): Mexican startup and tech coverage
- La República (Colombia): Colombian business and startup news

**Regulatory filings:**
- Brazil Receita Federal (receita.fazenda.gov.br): CNPJ lookup for Brazilian company data
- Mexico SAT (sat.gob.mx): RFC lookup, CFDI certification status
- Colombia Supersociedades: Registered company database, annual financial filings

**Investor portfolio pages:**
- Kaszek, Andreessen Horowitz LATAM, SoftBank LATAM, Monashees, Canary, Magma Partners,
  500 Latam, ALLVP, Ignia — portfolio pages reveal where VC money is flowing and which
  companies are considered fundable in each sector.

## 5. LATAM Competitive Context by Stage

| Stage | Competitive Dynamics |
|-------|---------------------|
| Pre-Seed | Competition is primarily from the substitute (incumbents, manual processes). Named competitors are usually larger and not targeting the exact beachhead. |
| Seed | First direct competitors emerge. Race to sign anchor customers before a competitor does. Distribution relationships matter more than product at this stage. |
| Series A | Competitive moats must be demonstrable. Investors will map the competitive landscape themselves — founders must show they have deeper knowledge of the competitive dynamics than the investor. |
| Series B+ | Expect US incumbents or well-funded international players to enter if the market is validated. Capital efficiency becomes the moat — who can grow faster per dollar deployed? |
